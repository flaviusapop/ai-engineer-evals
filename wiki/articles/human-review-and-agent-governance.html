Safety and governance

Human Review and Agent Governance

Covers approval design and governance ownership, not clinical intervention policy.

Download Markdown

On this page

  • Make approval a decision about a specific action
  • Preserve attention and independent judgment
  • Give responsibility a name

A human approval button is not, by itself, oversight. The person must know what consequential action is proposed, have evidence on which to challenge it, and retain authority to reject or change it. The system must also make clear who owns the agent and enforce the boundary around what it may do. A reviewer’s presence does not establish independent scrutiny. [1] [2]

Make approval a decision about a specific action

An approval should identify the approving person, agent or actor, session and run, tool, exact arguments and permitted lifetime, with its outcome linked to the action receipt. A click authorizing one action should not silently authorize a changed payload or an old retry. The proposed approval-state design in one speaker-reported case treats expiry as a terminating condition: a stale approved callback had remained retriable and blocked later work. The account does not provide a complete implementation or authorization schema. [3]

These records answer different questions. Permission authorizes a bounded action; an attempt shows the agent tried; a receipt records what a tool or system reported; an observation is a check of what could be seen; a verified outcome is a conclusion supported by checks against the intended state. Approval alone does not establish execution, and a receipt alone need not establish the intended lasting result. For outcome evidence, see Managing agent task flow and action evidence.

The approval screen should expose the distinction that matters, not merely announce that something consequential will happen. In a speaker-retold banking incident, approvers saw a warning that money would leave the bank—which was compatible with the intended interest payment—but missed that principal would leave too. Several people and a technically correct warning did not ensure the relevant invariant was checked. This anecdote motivates more specific evidence and warnings; it is not a test of an alternative interface. [4]

Give the reviewer the underlying source, uncertainty and an editable proposed action, rather than only a model recommendation and “approve.” A proposed document-exception workflow shifts from automatic filing to an analyst examining highlighted evidence and choosing whether to submit. It is a hypothetical design, not evidence of an approved banking deployment. In a separately reported financial-planning workflow, agents prepare drafts in parallel, but a planner reviews and adjusts every recommendation before client delivery. The reported boundary is useful, though the account does not measure review effectiveness. [1] [5]

Preserve attention and independent judgment

Reviewers can defer to automation even when they perform well on ordinary calibration. In selected historical legitimate sessions, proctors reportedly scored above 90% on calibration but accepted 50% of seeded fake signals. After guidance recast the signal as a preliminary alert and required independent video evidence for an adverse decision, reported rejection of fake signals rose from 50% to 71%. The speaker did not give a sample size or detailed design, so these figures do not establish a general effect. They do illustrate why measuring agreement alone is not enough: ask whether reviewers can find and use evidence that might contradict the alert. [2] [6]

Review is a scarce resource. Repeated routine prompts can encourage reflexive approval or “always allow,” especially when agents operate in parallel. Reserve deliberate friction for consequential, ambiguous or dangerous cases; make safe routine cases subject to an explicit bounded policy and deny clearly prohibited actions. This is a design tradeoff, not a universal threshold: estimate alert volume and ensure enough qualified reviewers have time to examine the evidence. Unnecessary friction on low-stakes work can also degrade the workflow. [7] [8] [9]

Written instructions to pause are useful but are not enforcement. A reported financial-advice workflow specifies draft-but-do-not-send as a limit; the description does not demonstrate technical enforcement. For consequential actions, put the approval boundary in the execution system rather than relying on the agent to remember it. Acknowledging a production freeze is likewise not proof that a destructive operation is impossible. The technical controls are covered in Agent Authority, Isolation and Runtime Controls; deciding when to widen autonomy belongs in Matching evidence and autonomy to risk. [10] [11]

Give responsibility a name

One proposed governance framework registers each agent under a stable identity and assigns a named human sponsor responsible for its purpose, costs and failures. The sponsor defines the relatively stable role: responsibilities, budget, intended outcomes and local rules. A runtime mission is the particular task or action being pursued now; an agent’s choice of mission must not silently redefine its role. Explicit ownership and handoff matter because a group of possible interveners is not necessarily a person who knows they are responsible. [12] [13] [14]

The sponsor can define local expectations, but inherits model behavior, external context and partner-agent behavior that may change outside their control. A named sponsor and registry make accountability traceable; they are not, on their own, an identity-security protocol or authorization mechanism. This is a conceptual framework, not an evaluated staffing model or a demonstrated reduction in failures. [15]
