Production evaluation and operation

Production governance, readiness and release

Release and accountability decisions, not detailed security threat controls. Owns readiness, explicit promotion criteria, judge stability and recovery checks; live monitoring is elsewhere. Owns exposure, accountability and runtime safeguards; evaluation methods are separate.

A green evaluation run is evidence for a release decision, not the decision itself and not deployment. A release owner must decide whether the evidence covers the intended use and its consequential failures; a separate deployment plan determines who is exposed, how behavior is observed, and how exposure can be stopped. A convincing demonstration alone cannot answer any of those questions.

Define target-specific thresholds and promotion blocks

Before comparing candidate versions, name the product outcome, its evaluation dataset, acceptable tradeoffs, and the failures that must block promotion. Compare configurations on a fixed set, not on a different selection of examples for each version. Keep the measured target clear: agreement between a judge and human labels measures alignment with that reference, not whether the application succeeds or is safe. An 80% agreement threshold in a judge-calibration demonstration is neither a universal launch floor nor a safety guarantee; the speaker's suggestion that perfect agreement necessarily signals overfitting is not established either. Early exploration may use informal feedback, but a scaled launch needs explicit standards. [1] [2]

Map high-consequence handoffs to substantive exit conditions and ensure the promotion mechanism actually blocks failure. For example, a ready flag and complete-looking content package can still contain an unsupported claim or violate the required voice; a demonstrated voice gate blocks the artifact rather than making it better. That demonstration supplies no measured detection performance. The fictional banking CI example runs evaluation and shows an earlier passing run, not automatic merging or proof of production safety. [3] [4] [5]

Read uncertainty and critical slices before claiming improvement

A positive average can hide a regression in a critical intent. Compare improvements and regressions on the same cases, report uncertainty around the net change, and separately examine consequential categories. One proposed rule requires the lower confidence bound on net gain to exceed zero; it is a decision rule, not a complete safety policy, and the source does not specify acceptable slice-level tolerances. Its HarborBite example and improvement counts are hypothetical, not production results. A favorable bound does not waive an independently blocking critical failure. [6] [7]

Stage exposure and prepare recovery

Tie each model, prompt, tool or other material change to repeatable evaluation evidence; then identify the human authorized to approve movement from staging into production. One described coding workflow builds and tests, but stops at staging for human approval; automated deployment was a future plan, not a demonstrated capability. [8] Separately, a team using scattered scripts proposed a standardized reusable evaluation harness as a future investment, not a completed capability. [9] See Learning from production evidence and regression tests for building regression suites.

Choose an initially supported use and a small pilot or beta cohort, with explicit criteria for expansion and a stable alternative. A proposed 5% prompt canary is a starting example, not a validated universal exposure or threshold. Increase reach only when observed behavior and the evidence appropriate to the risk justify it; traffic allocation itself establishes neither safety nor statistical significance. [10] [11]

Before exposure, agree on monitoring, who can halt or revert, and what recovery is actually possible. Reverting a configuration can limit future exposure but cannot undo every external action or harm already caused. For technical diagnosis, see Tracing and diagnosing production behavior. [12]

Version the system and validate in use

Record the execution recipe alongside each result: model and version, prompts, tools, runtime and relevant retrieval context. Preserve representative examples and use small reversible migrations. These records make a change investigable; they do not guarantee that hidden dependencies were captured or that another provider reproduces the same behavior. [13] [14]

An offline winner still needs comparison in the intended workflow. Allocate controlled live traffic, retain a baseline and watch user-relevant outcomes and latency within the agreed safeguards. Corrections can be weak signals and a user leaving is ambiguous, so neither proves success or failure by itself. A shown traffic-allocation interface is a control, not evidence of a significant benefit: its underlying metrics dashboard was unavailable in the cited account. [15] [16] [17]

Assign authority and audit what the system misses

For each consequential fork, write down the decision, authorized human role, required evidence and escalation route. Separate operational ownership from independent risk challenge and assurance. The daily operator needs practical power to interpret, override, reverse or stop the agent; log consequential approvals. In one illustrative workflow, the agent prepares variance analysis while designated people decide whether a shortfall is real, choose remediation and escalate a workstream. This is a governance illustration, not measured proof of effectiveness. [18] [19] [20] [21]

If reviewers see only flagged exceptions, independently sample unflagged work too. Review those cases for missed issues and their severity: a falling override rate might mean less scrutiny rather than a better system. For changes in delegated authority, see Matching evidence and autonomy to risk. [22]

Maintain an inventory of owners, dependencies, validation status and shutdown paths. Specify what triggers revalidation when models, APIs, infrastructure or data pipelines change, including shared changes with a wide blast radius. Functional regression and renewed threat assessment answer different questions; one does not replace the other. [23] [24] [25]

For claim-bearing outputs, make the supporting evidence reachable at the claim itself, not merely in a generic bibliography. The polished numerical assertion in the cited gate demonstration was deliberately unsupported; it is an example of what to block, not a real finding. [26] [27]

Make deployment dependencies and recovery evidence explicit

Before committing to architecture or a delivery date, map infrastructure, data, security, risk and approval prerequisites to named decision owners. A component may require several approvals and one decision may cover the entire system. A readiness score can prioritize unresolved questions, but it is a planning heuristic—not approval or a validated predictor of deployment success. A successful demo can still lack an approved use, operating environment or risk owner. [28] [29] [30]

Pin the evaluator model version and align the policy sources available to the agent and its judge so a policy update or silent evaluator change does not masquerade as application drift. Shared access does not ensure correct retrieval or interpretation, nor independent judgments: similar knowledge access and avoiding correlated model-family errors are separate goals. The retrieval demonstration updated one refund-policy document, not an enterprise-wide validated corpus. [31] [32]

Test restored data rather than relying on a successful backup or restore job. Compare table counts and check replica convergence, then assess what further verification the recovery objective requires: counts and convergence do not establish record-level semantic equality or a measured recovery time. Cross-region recovery can conflict with data-sovereignty requirements; separating metadata from core data is one proposed compromise, not an automatic compliance exemption. [33]

Decide risk and test operational controls

Assess severity by plausible harm, reversibility and blast radius, not by the size of a code change or the team's available delivery capacity. An accountable owner can decide to fix, delay or explicitly accept a risk where acceptance is authorized, recording deferred fast-follow work as a commitment. Existing accepted behavior can reveal inconsistent organizational standards, but does not establish that legacy harm is safe or that a legal or clinical risk is waivable. Post-launch observation cannot prevent an irreversible first exposure. [34] [35] [36] [37]

Manage prompt versions, tool access, routing, memory, autonomy and child-agent behavior as distinct control surfaces rather than one enabled switch. The proposed sequence starts with kill switches, defaults to suggestions, then introduces confirmation and opt-in automatic execution per tool; it is guidance, not a tested safety guarantee. Exercise switches and verify propagation to in-flight turns and child agents, cached responses, combinations of flags and audit records. Track switch activation, mitigation time, canary error differences and completeness of change records; tune suggested thresholds to context. A switch cannot reverse completed side effects. The source inconsistently counts the surfaces, so its count is not a control specification. For enforcement mechanics, see Agent Authority, Isolation and Runtime Controls. [38] [39] [40] [41]

Automated oversight may inspect incoming context, behavior and work products at workflow speed while humans define and revise rules. This is a proposed architecture, not a measured guardian accuracy or a transfer of accountability. Likewise, an incident-agent demo tailored to one scenario does not establish reliability across teams: evaluate relevant context, permissions, causal reasoning and coordination across incidents before broad deployment. [42] [43]

Related concepts

  • Managing agent task flow and action evidence

    Own task progression, action evidence and handoffs; not general-purpose evaluation metrics or permission policy.

  • Running and resuming agent sessions

    Own session lifecycle, persistence and event execution; distinguish working-session state from curated organizational memory.

  • Choosing agent delegation and coordination

    Own topology, specialist coordination and capacity tradeoffs; link model portfolio selection to routing-and-deployment.

  • Designing agent-facing product interactions

    Own interaction surfaces and application-facing workflows; not the tool catalog or authorization contract.

  • Agent Authority, Isolation and Runtime Controls

    Own permissions, execution and emergency control; privacy retention and tenancy have their own article.

  • Tool interfaces

    Tool affordances and invocation, schema discovery and progressive loading of instructions.

  • Tracing and diagnosing production behavior

    Evidence capture and root-cause investigation, not a proof of user-visible success. Owns trace collection, privacy, triage, attribution and cost; it does not itself decide release readiness. Owns live discovery and incident handling; release gates and memory maintenance are separate.

  • Delivering agent-assisted work with human review

    Owns software-delivery practices and adoption; runtime controls and evaluation infrastructure are separate. Review capacity, decisions and handoffs rather than test-suite construction.

  • Operating secure, governed agent environments

    Security-specific threats and controls, distinct from general release decisions. Workload execution, access and infrastructure testing rather than semantic product quality.

  • Matching evidence and autonomy to risk

    Focus on system risk, harm prioritization and deployment decisions; detailed technical enforcement belongs in agent controls.
