Judges and evaluators

Choose and combine judge methods

Evaluator architectures and process grading, not their continuing operations.

Download Markdown

On this page

  • Separate production, review and review signals
  • Match the grader to the property
  • Separate outcome from trajectory
  • Judge open-ended reasoning and adaptive paths
  • Choose an ensemble rule for the decision

A judge architecture should follow the property being checked, not the other way around. A parser, a model critic, an expert and a tool-equipped investigator can all contribute, but they answer different questions and introduce different errors. Keep their results separate long enough to understand what each actually establishes.

Separate production, review and review signals

An agent’s statement that it completed a task is not a completion check. Give a reviewer a distinct fault-finding job and fresh context: the artifact and relevant original evidence, without the producer’s drafting history or conclusions when the aim is to avoid anchoring. A reported demand-letter review used opposing legal perspectives to find a renewal clause while a separate auditor checked arithmetic. These were agent personas, not demonstrated professional legal review. [1]

A reviewer can be another model, but a different model or context does not make errors statistically independent: reviewers can share data, assumptions and blind spots. Nor does an objection automatically justify a change; check criticisms against their sources, and keep approval authority separate from review. A booking demonstration separates execution, validation and approval, but its selected cases do not establish that this eliminates correlated errors. [2]

You can also keep review signals blind until synthesis. For example, one branch examines design quality while another collects mechanical defects; the synthesizer then considers both. Otherwise a large defect count can anchor the qualitative review, while an empty page can look excellent merely because it has no detectable polish violations. This architecture was described by its author; the intended subagent execution was not clearly shown live. [3]

Match the grader to the property

Use a repeatable code check when the requirement and reliable evidence can be expressed directly: JSON parsing, a required field, an unambiguous tool argument or an authorization limit. Use expert or model judgment for meaning that cannot be captured reliably that way. A tool-equipped judge can investigate an open-ended case, but extra model calls add cost and require their own validation. [4]

Deterministic does not mean comprehensive. In a workbook example, the same inputs are supplied to a reference and generated workbook and their outputs compared, testing behavior without demanding identical formulas. The example does not specify input coverage, tolerance or engine fidelity, so a pass cannot establish correctness for every spreadsheet. [5] A contract team reported that its model judge introduced substantial noise, but did not quantify it; that experience supports using reliable code checks where available, not assuming all legal meaning is mechanical. [6]

Conversely, a keyword may measure the wrong property. In a constructed returns example, requiring “cannot” also passes an acceptance that says the customer cannot pay shipping. In a separate waitlist demonstration, persisted state does not make a message promising a confirmed seat true. Check persistence directly and assess whether the message matches the business outcome; neither demonstration establishes semantic-judge accuracy. [7] [8] Likewise, OCR can help check required rendered text while aesthetic quality may still need human judgment; OCR can miss errors too. [9]

Keep dimensions visible: a summary can have valid JSON, structurally valid references, faithful content, appropriate length and acceptable latency, each with a different failure mode. Combining methods does not validate their references or cover every possible error. For designing the criteria and testing judge alignment, see Turn task quality into actionable evaluation rubrics and Validate judges against human labels. [10]

Separate outcome from trajectory

A final result and the path to it are different evaluands. A correct-looking artifact obtained by escaping a sandbox to inspect hidden tests is not a valid success. Conversely, an agent that searches correctly but cannot buy an out-of-stock item may deserve process credit even though the purchase outcome remains unsuccessful. Checking the trajectory is a detection aid, not a replacement for isolation or authorization controls. [11] [12]

Avoid making one arbitrary action sequence the definition of success when alternative paths are legitimate. Score essential constraints and relevant intermediate work separately. For instance, a task can penalize choosing the wrong person while still crediting a correct report about the person actually chosen; partial credit does not turn the overall answer into a success. Nor should an invented requirement be added merely because it sounds useful. [13] [14] [15]

This distinction also matters for training feedback: in a simplified outcome-reward illustration, giving every step the final outcome’s positive reward can reinforce a wrong intermediate step. Step-level supervision can distinguish it, but human labeling is costly and model-generated labels introduce verifier errors. This illustration is not a complete account of reinforcement learning. [16]

Judge open-ended reasoning and adaptive paths

When competent people can reach different conclusions, assess whether the necessary evidence and reasoning support the conclusion rather than forcing a single golden recommendation. An investment benchmark describes this approach for different defensible calls, but its results are preliminary and its rubric reliability was not measured. [17] A separate attribution workflow has analysts produce evidence-backed opinions and a synthesis judge weigh their reasoning; if agreement is insufficient, it may expand the jury. The source supplies no threshold, stopping rule or validation. Consensus is an input to a decision, not proof of a uniquely true causal allocation. [18] [19]

Appropriate uncertainty can itself be a target: one team reports penalizing shaky assumptions and encouraging a clarifying question for ambiguous document fields. Asking also imposes user work; the source does not measure the costs of asking too often or too rarely. [20]

For a highly variable execution, a tool-equipped judge can inspect the observed trajectory and choose further checks—for example, following a suspicious handoff to inspect its tool arguments. This is a per-case investigation, not cross-trace discovery, and its findings are hypotheses to verify. Proposed adaptive judges supplement rather than replace fixed checks and ordinary model judges; superior discovery of unknown failures has not been established in a controlled comparison. [21] [22]

Choose an ensemble rule for the decision

Specify what a positive verdict means before combining votes. Requiring multiple reviewers to agree before raising a finding can suppress noisy alerts, but may also suppress real defects when any reviewer misses them. A speaker described such multipass review and cited separate research reporting improvement, without establishing the false-negative cost. [23]

For accepting training examples, requiring every judge to approve is restrictive in a different direction: it can reject useful data. In a vision-labeling demonstration, one small judge rejected heavily, so the presenter accepted an example if either judge approved, preserving more data at a possible quality cost. Validation was incomplete and the source’s recall remarks are ambiguous. [24] Neither rule is universally best: compare missed harms, false alerts and lost coverage for the actual decision, and remember shared errors can defeat a voting scheme. Agreement is not truth.
