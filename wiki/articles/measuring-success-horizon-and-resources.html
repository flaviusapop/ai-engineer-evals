Agent evaluations

Choosing agent success measures and resource budgets

Defines distinct estimands and accounting; not experimental validity or task construction.

Download Markdown

On this page

  • Repeated attempts: capability, reliability and revision
  • Horizons and the variation around a score
  • Aggregate outcomes without hiding progress
  • Resource and interaction accounting

An agent evaluation can answer several different questions: whether a task is solvable at all, whether it works repeatedly, how much of a long task it completes, and what resources it consumes. None of those answers is a substitute for the others. Start with the decision the measurement must support, then state the task population, attempt policy, success criterion and denominator alongside the result.

Repeated attempts: capability, reliability and revision

For each task, run the same configuration multiple times. Any-success asks whether at least one of the allowed attempts succeeds; report the number of tasks with at least one success divided by the number of tasks evaluated. This is useful when a person can afford retries and reliably identify a good candidate. All-run success asks whether every attempt for a task succeeds; its denominator is again the number of tasks, but its numerator counts only tasks successful on every attempt. It is more relevant to recurring unattended work. The fraction of individual attempts that succeed is a third quantity: its denominator is attempts, not tasks. Report the attempt count per task and whether the denominator includes timeouts and other failed runs. [1] [2]

These summaries change as the number of attempts changes, so compare them only at the same attempt budget. An observed all-run pass is not a guarantee of future operation. One source informally describes a consistency metric as how often repeated runs succeed; its captioned notation does not justify redefining it as an all-run estimator. [3]

A feedback-guided revision loop is a different experiment. Here each attempt can use the previous critique, so measure the fraction of tasks reaching acceptance within the specified revision budget, as well as first-attempt and final outcomes and the feedback provided. In a described image-editing example, a portion-size and plating critique informed a second attempt reported to pass. That demonstration illustrates a sequential process; it does not supply an independent-sampling formula or a general success curve. [4]

Horizons and the variation around a score

A human-relative task horizon associates the duration a human would take on benchmark tasks with a specified agent success probability. A 50% horizon is not the length of task the agent reliably completes every time. State the threshold, benchmark task mix and how human duration is defined. One speaker interpreted external plots as roughly 16–18 hours at 50% versus about 3.5 hours at 80%; those are approximate, speaker-reported and model-specific, not independently established deployment guarantees. Even 80% may be insufficient for the application. [5] [6]

Retries can increase the chance of at least one success under an independent-attempt assumption, but repeated failures may be correlated and success is useful only if a correct result can be selected. A horizon threshold, observed all-run success and a revision-budget result therefore answer different questions. [7]

Repeat evaluations to expose score and trajectory variability, not just to find a favorable run. Report how many repetitions were made, their spread, what was held fixed and any uncertainty analysis; repeated semantic agreement still does not establish truth. One speaker reported variation up to plus or minus 20% across reruns of 65 benchmarks under the same model, harness and infrastructure. This was a maximum reported observation with incomplete scale and sampling details, not a universal error bar. [8] [9]

A candidate research record raises a related but distinct question: is the apparent improvement larger than training randomness? The reported agent-driven research workflow submits candidate changes for statistical validation before accepting records. Predefine the comparison and validation threshold appropriate to the experiment; the account does not specify its statistical test, sample size or threshold, so it cannot prescribe one. [10]

Aggregate outcomes without hiding progress

A verifier produces a result for an individual rollout. An evaluation aggregates over attempts and tasks; say whether every rollout or every task has equal weight, and disclose the intended task set, exclusions and incomplete runs. A successful one-task smoke test can check that the pipeline works, but is not a full-set score. Slices matter: in one speaker-reported KiCad benchmark, the strongest agent fully passed six of 25 tasks, all edits of existing schematics; none of the blank-schematic tasks succeeded. The split reveals a boundary that the overall fraction alone hides. Configurations and uncertainty were not supplied, and full-pass rate is distinct from reward. [11] [12] [13]

On long or difficult tasks, final completion can be rare and slow to observe. Record intermediate submissions or independently checkable milestones to show where a trajectory reached, with the count reaching each milestone over the relevant attempted tasks. In an access-control benchmark, intermediate discovery and exploitation checks distinguish partial progress from the final unauthorized action. Neither an intermediate pass nor a later submission proves final success. Stopping after an early failed stage may save resources, but can change the measured task if recovery was possible or the stage boundary was wrong. [14] [15] [16]

Resource and interaction accounting

Present outcome quality alongside elapsed time, active time, token use, attempts and human interventions, rather than treating them as interchangeable. Active-time normalization can make activity rates comparable, but also report the elapsed-time denominator and idle periods: unattended users pay for waiting and restarts too. In a preliminary, informal research-agent comparison, one agent reportedly needed repeated human prompts and was idle for about a third of its time; model and harness were confounded. Rankings also changed when progress was plotted against output tokens instead of elapsed days, and full cost accounting was unavailable. [17] [18]

A deadline defines which attempts can finish and how much unproductive searching can occur. A generous budget may avoid penalizing a slower endpoint, but may also permit costly loops; disclose the timeout and stopping behavior rather than treating a particular deadline as universally fair. If time breaks a tie, verify start and end events, clock resolution and handling of implausible values. One workshop leaderboard displayed apparent zero-second completions that organizers said should not be possible, leaving the speed ordering uncertain. [19] [20]

Finally, completed outcomes can still impose needless questions or steps on a user. Record interaction burden separately from completion and required safeguards: an extra step is not automatically wrong, and the cited benchmark gives neither a universal shortest-path oracle nor penalty weights. A longer route may be appropriate when it obtains necessary clarification. [21] [22]

For the design of a fair comparison, see Comparing agents without confounding the result; for the underlying outcome checks and diagnostic traces, see Verifying agent outcomes and diagnosing behavior.
