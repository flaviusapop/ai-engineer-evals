Production evaluation and operation

Delivering agent-assisted work with human review

Owns software-delivery practices and adoption; runtime controls and evaluation infrastructure are separate. Review capacity, decisions and handoffs rather than test-suite construction.

Download Markdown

On this page

  • Adapt the workflow to the project
  • Decide what to build before delegating
  • Make work reviewable and keep it steerable
  • Shorten feedback without confusing it with verification
  • Measure the delivery system, not just generation

An agent can produce a change faster than a team can decide whether it should exist, understand it, or release it. A workable delivery process therefore starts with intent and ends with an accountable decision—not with a generated pull request. Adapt the amount of automation and review to the project, the evidence available, and the consequences of being wrong.

Adapt the workflow to the project

A harness demonstrated on a new application may assume a particular stack, design system and set of checks. In an established repository, test it against the actual build, conventions and change constraints before expanding its remit. Starting with monitoring, issue creation and reviewable pull requests can be more appropriate than handing over whole-application generation. The cited greenfield pattern was not validated as a long-lived brownfield deployment. Its speakers favored reducing recurring human intervention, while audience members wanted sprint reviews; stop-and-resume hooks are a proposed alternative, not a settled answer for every project. [1] [2]

Invite skeptical maintainers to identify what generated changes routinely miss: undocumented dependencies, project conventions, or tests that cannot expose a particular regression. Give them ownership of a roadmap to close those gaps rather than treating their reluctance as an adoption problem. They may already be absorbing the integration burden created by faster generation. This is organizational experience and advice, not evidence that the approach caused adoption to rise. [3] [4]

Decide what to build before delegating

Start the brief with the user problem and why the change matters. Investigate the current system and ask only clarification questions whose answers could change the work. A reviewed plan records the intended behavior, constraints, proposed scope and unresolved decisions so that implementation can proceed without losing the purpose. One team describes substantial up-front planning; that is a local practice, not a required duration. A separate experiment reported improved repeatability from targeted clarification across tested agents, but did not establish a universal prompt or guarantee. [5] [6] [7]

When no specification exists, observe the work. In a hypothetical document-exception assistant, shadow an analyst to learn which systems are consulted, where judgment and permission enter, and what happens when a document is missing. Draft a workflow naming accessible documents, permitted processing, output, reviewer and submitter; take it to sponsors and control owners for correction. Agree on success against the current process rather than inventing a precise error target from the source's garbled caption. [8]

Choose the smallest useful increment with a credible approval path. Summarizing material an analyst already holds, reading a second system, drafting customer-facing text and sending it automatically are different scopes with different review questions. Existing access does not automatically authorize a new use, and small scope is not an exemption from controls. [9]

Make work reviewable and keep it steerable

Record the work on shared, durable surfaces: an issue describes the request, an agent run produces a pull request, and reviewers leave corrections on that change. Versioned team workflows make later inspection and improvement possible; visibility alone is neither proof of safe automation nor permission to disclose the record publicly. A low-friction way to introduce agents is to ask for a bounded task in an existing team conversation and have the agent report back in the same thread. The cited organization still had unresolved build-system dependencies for cloud agents. [10] [11]

Label agent-written changes and distinguish a short human-authored explanation of intent from generated supporting detail. This is a useful local convention, not a universal compliance rule. The initiating person remains responsible for the handoff and remediation. Tests, logs and review artifacts are evidence; an accountable person or organization still decides whether to ship, block, or accept risk. An automatically triggered first draft does not itself have authority to merge. The responsible owner should be able to explain the intent, evidence and risks without necessarily reading or typing every line; human approval itself is not proof of safety. [12] [13] [14] [15] [16]

For long or parallel workstreams, maintain checkpoints and evidence as work progresses rather than asking a reviewer to reconstruct a day of decisions from a final summary. A green test suite can coexist with a growing gap between the code in the system and what the team understands. Review needs to preserve comprehension as well as find defects. [17] [18]

Give each consumer a usable review surface: humans need readable findings, rationale and links to the rules applied; an agent revising the work needs structured findings and repair context. A proposed alternative groups changes around intent and demonstrated outcomes, such as a feature video. It may conserve attention, but a video or automated review can miss hidden implementation defects; neither approach has demonstrated equivalent defect detection in the cited material. Use potential blast radius to prioritize review, not as a proven threshold for bypassing it. [19] [20] [21]

When multiple automated loops make PRs, route a designated review comment, the diff and relevant discussion only to the loop responsible for that PR. Labels can support routing; they do not by themselves authorize comments or defend against malicious instructions. Review approvals and revision requests can also become feedback for improving the workflow, subject to human interpretation. Return classified developer replies, action rates and traces to the teams that own local review rules so they can revise unhelpful ones. Addressing a comment or expressing sentiment is not independent proof that a finding was correct. [22] [23] [24] [25] [26]

Keep operational intervention possible as well: a research interface can allow people to cancel or add experiments, inspect links between ideas and outcomes, and receive escalation on failure. Telemetry-informed self-correction is a proposal, not evidence of a safe action policy. If a conversation is converted into proposed product changes, ground it in the relevant application and let the user review before applying. One wearable demonstration used preloaded data; a repository-aware backlog integration was unfinished, so neither establishes reliable autonomous backlog creation. [27] [28] [29] [30] [31]

Shorten feedback without confusing it with verification

A quick prototype can support a product conversation even when its code is disposable. For a desktop product, one team created a web shell and per-PR preview links so colleagues could inspect variants without each installing a local build. That offers a faster shared feedback surface, but it does not establish coverage of native desktop behavior. Reusable accessible components can similarly make a fix available in several places; each use still needs contextual accessibility verification. [32] [33] [34]

Layer verification: provide project guidance before generation, give the agent feedback during implementation, use CI checks before integration, and maintain the codebase after delivery. These layers address different opportunities to prevent, detect and repair problems; installing a set of tools is not proof that they work. Embedding governance in a harness is a proposed architecture, not demonstrated compliance. For the detailed mechanics of gates see Building verification feedback and integration gates; for enforcement of authority see Agent Authority, Isolation and Runtime Controls. [35] [36]

After preview and approval, observe the deployed behavior. VS Code describes staged releases monitored through errors and issues, particularly useful when rolling back installed software is costly. A separate agent-watched rollout is an emerging proposal. Neither account supplies a validated abort threshold or measured incident reduction; decide in advance who can react and how. [37] [38]

Operational failures can feed reviewed changes: filter and group related error stacks into owned issues, let an agent investigate and propose a fix, and provide a way for people to correct mistaken triage. One reported VS Code pipeline follows this pattern. A proposed voice-agent loop would replay failed traces against a development fix and present the result for approval; it was imagined future work, not a completed autonomous repair experiment. [39] [40] [41]

Measure the delivery system, not just generation

Faster code production can move the queue into design, review, testing, security or recovery. Track the flow from user feedback through decision, validation, release and learning, including the burden on reviewers. A code-review vendor reported widely varying PR throughput among its users, but more PRs do not establish more useful or safe functionality. [42] [43] [44]

Treat activity measures as proxies. Committed agent-written code measures acceptance, not correctness; a reported increase coincided with model and harness changes. Inferred authorship depends on labeling and the vendor's customer mix. Review rounds depend on task scope and reviewer behavior; the captioned numerical comparison is corrupted. Addressed comments measure action, and unreviewed merges measure adoption of a workflow—not safe outcomes. Compare these signals with independently observed defects and product outcomes rather than treating any one as a quality verdict. [45] [46] [47] [48]

Related concepts

  • Managing agent task flow and action evidence

    Own task progression, action evidence and handoffs; not general-purpose evaluation metrics or permission policy.

  • Running and resuming agent sessions

    Own session lifecycle, persistence and event execution; distinguish working-session state from curated organizational memory.

  • Choosing agent delegation and coordination

    Own topology, specialist coordination and capacity tradeoffs; link model portfolio selection to routing-and-deployment.

  • Designing agent-facing product interactions

    Own interaction surfaces and application-facing workflows; not the tool catalog or authorization contract.

  • Agent Authority, Isolation and Runtime Controls

    Own permissions, execution and emergency control; privacy retention and tenancy have their own article.

  • Tool interfaces

    Tool affordances and invocation, schema discovery and progressive loading of instructions.

  • Build and maintain production-grounded evaluation sets

    Collection, refresh and incident regression rather than synthetic generation.

  • Evaluation infrastructure and team ownership

    Owns human responsibilities and reusable experimentation infrastructure, not specific monitoring choices. Owns team responsibilities and architectural capacity, not individual launch approvals.

  • Monitoring production quality and improvement

    Operational measurement and uncertainty, not human decision rights. Owns quality signals, test evolution, risk and task outcomes; execution traces and platform plumbing are covered elsewhere. Observability, discovery and continuous evaluation rather than offline release authorization.

  • Maintaining agent context, memory and knowledge

    Knowledge and retrieval state, not transactional execution guarantees. Owns retention, retrieval and maintenance of working knowledge; external-source provenance is separate. Procedural knowledge and recurring jobs, not generic trace storage. Owns reusable knowledge lifecycle; working-session context belongs to execution.
