Judges and evaluators

Operate and improve evaluation systems

Maintenance, triage and feedback loops, not initial architecture design.

Download Markdown

On this page

  • Turn verdicts into bounded action
  • Spend human attention on disagreement—and on passes
  • Maintain a changing judge without losing comparability
  • Select and improve the judge on its actual task

An evaluation system is not finished when its judge produces a score. The score must help someone decide what to inspect or change, and the judge itself can become inaccurate as tasks, user behavior, and standards change. Operate it as a monitored component, not as a source of unquestionable ground truth.

Turn verdicts into bounded action

Keep the dimension, verdict, and reason together. An advertisement can be acceptable for brand safety yet factually inaccurate; a single fail would conceal which property needs repair. A rater's explanation can point a developer toward the offending claim or missing context. A model-generated explanation is useful evidence to inspect, not proof that the verdict or the explanation is correct. [1] [2]

Use that feedback to direct a specific revision, then assess the revised artifact again. A refund agent, for example, can be checked against the store's eligibility rubric, rather than a vague helpfulness criterion. Set a maximum number of attempts and an escalation or stop condition; a retry loop consumes resources and can repeatedly optimize against the same faulty grader. The refund example does not establish grader accuracy or convergence. A separate demonstration uses an overall session rubric and bounded retries, but its prior financial scorecard was not independently verified, and its session-level outcome was not an individual grade for each delegated subtask. [3] [4] [5]

Spend human attention on disagreement—and on passes

Use disagreements between the agent, verifiers, or distinct judges to prioritize cases where a domain expert can resolve ambiguity or find a defective criterion. Production signals such as a possible frustrated follow-up can also find candidates, but a flag is not a confirmed failure. One support-workshop example interprets a follow-up using conversation history; it was staged and did not validate the disagreement label. [6] [7]

Do not spend the whole review budget on flagged cases. In one vendor case study, all reported errors among 200 held-out families fell within six disagreements, but a separate example had four model families share the same wrong sign convention. In a generated clinical-note exercise, the presenter reported serious errors in one in five apparent clean passes, often omissions; its denominator, labeling method, configuration and uncertainty were not supplied. Neither result is a general error rate. Together they explain why agreement and passing verdicts also deserve sampled review, including targeted checks for known shared blind spots. A selectively reviewed queue cannot by itself estimate overall prevalence. [8] [9] [10]

Human reviewers should also use the product and inspect complete traces, not just the judge's final label. An apparently poor answer may arise from failed retrieval, a bad tool call, or reasoning after a correct tool result; the repair differs. For the distinction between agreement and truth, see Agreement, truth and the limits of evaluator proxies. [11] [12]

Maintain a changing judge without losing comparability

Regularly compare sampled human and judge decisions and inspect reasons, including examples from new user populations and failure types. Record the task, input distribution, judge/model and rubric versions with results. When experts reveal a missing criterion, update the rubric and relevant examples, then revalidate against independently held-out judgments reflecting the new standard. These are practical safeguards, not a prescribed numerical threshold: the cited teams did not disclose a universal sampling rate, agreement target, or complete calibration protocol. [13] [14]

A stable rubric is useful for comparing implementations; an evolving rubric is necessary when the old one misses what matters. Preserve the old version or run both versions on a common set when feasible, and label the break: a score increase after changing the definition does not on its own show the agent improved. A clinical-note presenter reported qualitatively better performance from a judge updated with expert feedback on the same generated-note dataset, but did not provide split hygiene, exact scores or prospective outcomes. Treat the loop as a hypothesis to validate, not proof that iterative feedback generalizes. [15] [16]

Select and improve the judge on its actual task

Start with a clear property and a simple baseline judge on relevant traces. Inspect its errors with domain reviewers; test whether a capable model can do the task before trying cheaper models and adapting their instructions. Compare candidates on independent human-labeled cases, including important error types and cost, not on a generic leaderboard alone. A reported legal trace-judging collaboration used this capability-first approach, but disclosed neither detailed cost accounting nor judge-agreement data. Prompt inspection makes a proposed change reviewable; it does not validate it. [17] [18] [19]

Feedback volume depends on the task and what is being trained. One vendor describes small batches of roughly 10–20 human examples to improve a judge and larger production feedback sets for reward models; that is not a universal sample-size rule. Keep examples used to optimize the judge or agent separate from the independent evaluation used to decide promotion. Otherwise a system may learn to satisfy its own reviewer while both repeat the same mistake. See Choose and combine judge methods for method selection and Protect holdouts and historical evidence for holdout protection. [20] [21]

    Human labeling and evidence quality rather than test-distribution design.

  • Give judges the evidence and context they need

    Evidence access and verification, not judge error-rate estimation.

  • Turn task quality into actionable evaluation rubrics

    Criteria and score labels, not comparative estimands or evaluator architecture.
