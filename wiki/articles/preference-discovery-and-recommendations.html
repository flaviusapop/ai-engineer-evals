Domain evaluation

Preference discovery and recommendations

Covers preference discovery and recommendations; other articles own their distinct mechanisms and evaluation targets.

Download Markdown

On this page

  • Discover the preference without exhausting the user
  • Turn preferences into catalog choices and assess fit

A person looking for a sofa may know that a room should feel calm without knowing the catalog's style vocabulary. A recommender must help turn that incomplete intent into a usable choice, while keeping stated requirements separate from guesses. A click can inform the process, but it does not by itself show that the item fits, that the user liked it, or that the explanation was true. [1] [2]

Discover the preference without exhausting the user

Start with a working state containing explicit facts and constraints, such as a budget, separately from inferred preferences, such as the style suggested by an inspiration image. Record uncertainty about the inference rather than promoting it to a fact. Evaluate whether supplied details survive in state and are represented correctly. The shopping system describes confidence within an error bound, but provides neither the bound nor a calibration method; a confidence field is not evidence that its probabilities are reliable. [3]

Ask about the unknown most likely to block a useful choice. For a living-room redesign, room width can matter before refining a style preference: a sofa that cannot fit is not a viable recommendation. This is an information-value objective, not a published numerical estimator or proven optimal questioning policy. Evaluate both useful constraint discovery and the burden of repeated or inconsequential questions. If the user can state a dimension plainly, text may suffice; if they cannot articulate a visual style, a board of examples can provide shared reference points. Hovers and clicks are tentative signals, not validated evidence of liking. [4] [5]

Test updates as well as retention. Illustrative example: a shopper changes the maximum width but not the budget. The extracted width should change and the budget should remain stable; a state that rewrites both is not faithfully tracking the revision. The source describes this counterfactual logic, not a dataset construction method or passing threshold. [6]

A simulator seeded with constraints hidden from the agent can test which constraints the agent elicits and how many turns it takes. It can expose repeated questions even if a final recommendation looks plausible. But the described simulator was not validated against real shoppers, and neither coverage of hidden preferences nor a recovery rate was reported. Real-user evidence remains necessary. A speaker also reported that users wanted involvement in exploring possibilities, while price comparison or negotiation might be delegated; the study's size and protocol were not supplied, and agent-to-agent shopping was not yet built. Treat this as a design hypothesis, not a universal automation preference. [7] [8]

Let people inspect and correct inferred taste. In a reported music example, listening on a shared device included the speaker's children's Disney music; an editable natural-language profile could distinguish that history from his own preferences and express future aspirations. This makes user correction possible, but the account does not report correction accuracy or long-term effects. Behavior can ground an inference without being the user's unquestionable intent. [9]

Turn preferences into catalog choices and assess fit

A preference such as “calm and compact” must be mapped to catalog attributes before it can constrain retrieval. The proposed shopping agent bridges conversation and merchant ontology; merchant expertise can clarify what the local metadata actually means. Evaluate whether the mapping preserves the user's constraint and whether eligible items are retrieved, rather than assuming that a plausible translation is correct. No mapping accuracy or fairness result is supplied. [10]

Catalog representation is a different concern from learning preference. In a Spotify architecture account, existing content embeddings are quantized into discrete semantic-ID tokens added to a model's vocabulary. A request and listening-history IDs can then yield an entity ID plus a natural-language explanation. This helps connect language generation to catalog entities; emitting a valid ID does not establish that the recommendation fits the user or that the explanation is faithful. [11]

Assess distinct parts of the response rather than relying on engagement alone:

  • Fit: Does the item satisfy explicit constraints and the user's current intent, including corrections to inferred taste?
  • Fidelity: Are product details and the reasons given for the recommendation supported by the available information?
  • Presentation and actionability: Can the person find the relevant trade-offs and decide what to do next? A comparison table, a policy summary or visual inspiration may help in different situations; none is mandatory for every query.
  • Interaction: Did the process uncover the important blocker without redundant questions or obscuring the user's choice?

A purchase-oriented actionability measure should not be presented as proof of causal conversion or user welfare. The described recommenders use or propose language-model raters for stage-specific qualities, but the shopping account provides no rater prompts, calibration or longitudinal improvement evidence. Ground judgments in meaningful user evidence and human preferences, while recognizing that behavioral history can be misleading; validate the judges rather than treating them as automatic authorities. See Validate judges against human labels. [12] [13] [14] [2]
