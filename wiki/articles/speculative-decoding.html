Efficiency and performance

Speculative decoding

Covers token-level speculation, not speculative voice pipeline execution.

Download Markdown

On this page

  • Draft and verify token blocks
  • Choose a drafter and measure net benefit
  • Compare the loaded service with its baseline

Speculative decoding can make generation faster by using a cheaper process to propose future tokens and asking the target model to check a block at once. It is an optimization of token generation, not an independent check of whether the answer is factually correct. Whether it helps depends on accepted work and the cost of producing and checking it.

Draft and verify token blocks

In ordinary autoregressive generation, the model advances one token at a time. With a draft-model approach, a smaller model proposes several next tokens; the target evaluates the candidates together, accepts suitable ones and corrects rejected work before generation continues. One speaker gives three to five draft tokens per cycle as a typical illustrative setting, not a universal optimum. The opportunity is to advance through several useful tokens with fewer expensive sequential target steps. [1] [2]

This is target verification of token proposals, not verification against external evidence. Nor does this high-level account specify the acceptance and rejection algorithm needed to establish identical sampling distributions under every decoding setting. A speaker’s assertion that quality is unchanged is not a substitute for a distribution-preservation argument or a comparative quality experiment. Check the implementation and its validity for the intended sampling settings separately from the speed measurement.

Token speculation is also different from overlapping stages of a workflow or a voice pipeline: the latter may begin useful work before another stage finishes, whereas this mechanism proposes token continuations for target verification. For user-facing readiness and first audible speech, see Interactive latency.

Choose a drafter and measure net benefit

A draft must be cheap enough to pay for itself and compatible enough with the target to produce useful candidates. A tiny model that is frequently rejected wastes its own work and still incurs target checking; a more accurate but expensive drafter can also eliminate the saving. One speaker recommends a much smaller model with the same tokenizer and ideally the same model family. A suggested size ratio is guidance for starting experiments, not a proven optimum. [3] [4]

There are other ways to propose tokens. A model-based drafter adds model compute and may improve acceptance; a model-free strategy can reuse patterns from the prompt or earlier output with lower overhead, especially when the continuation resembles that context. Compare actual net cost, not acceptance alone. Acceptance is the fraction of proposed tokens the target accepts; it explains how much speculative work survived, but is not itself latency, throughput or output quality. [5] [6]

Task and generation settings matter. Code, JSON and SQL are plausible candidates for better agreement than less predictable creative continuations, but one demonstration changed temperature as well as task type. It therefore does not isolate the effect of task structure. A drafter that works on coding may fail on specialized prose; remeasure when either the workload or sampling settings change. [7] [8]

Compare the loaded service with its baseline

Compare speculation on and off using representative prompts, output lengths, sampling settings and traffic. Record accepted draft fraction, generation speed, user-visible latency, throughput and resource cost together. Drafting, target verification and rejected proposals all consume time. The additional model and caches also require memory; under higher concurrency they can compete with capacity needed for other requests. A single-request improvement need not translate into a better loaded service. [9] [10]

The evidence illustrates both directions, not a universal expected gain. A structured example on a Blackwell setup reportedly generated about 1.6 times faster; it was a single demonstration without repeated trials or a throughput curve. In a different demonstration, generation at concurrency one fell from about 58 to 16 tokens per second. The speaker attributed the loss primarily to draft incompatibility, but did not isolate that cause experimentally. [6] [4]

A vendor describes adapting draft strategy to request and workload because per-request gains can impose validation overhead on a batch. That is a reasonable decision to investigate, not a separately ablated proof that adaptive speculation wins. Use Measuring inference serving performance to interpret service throughput and latency, and measure the complete application outcome separately: faster token production does not verify a report’s sources or establish its time to usable completion. [11] [9]
