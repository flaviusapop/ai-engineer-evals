Foundations

What evaluation can establish: design, evidence and uncertainty

Teaches evaluation structure, evidence scope, uncertainty in claims and evaluation maturity; specific experimental estimators and product economics belong elsewhere. Owns control, sampling, uncertainty and causal interpretation; does not teach item-response models.

    • Check the measuring instrument

An AI evaluation is a structured way to collect evidence about a system performing specified tasks. It can inform a decision without proving that the system will succeed on every future case—or that a measured improvement caused a business outcome. The question is always: what was measured, under which conditions, and how far does that evidence travel?

Build an interpretable measurement

An evaluation combines cases, a runnable system and its environment, a description of acceptable behavior, a scoring or judging method, and a way to inspect or aggregate results. A support-agent case might provide a customer request and account state, run the agent with its tools, and assess both its reply and the resulting account action. An expectation can describe an acceptable policy-consistent response rather than one exact sentence. Even a small loop and spreadsheet can make outputs inspectable; the format does not itself establish representative coverage or a reliable grader. [1] [2]

A benchmark is a reusable set of such tasks and scoring rules. Ask whether its tasks and standards reflect the capability of interest (validity), whether results are sufficiently consistent across relevant repetitions (reliability), and whether scores can distinguish the candidates (discrimination). If all systems hit the floor or ceiling, a benchmark may be unable to discriminate even if its tasks look relevant. A popular benchmark can still reward a shortcut; common use is not evidence of validity. [3] [4]

The result belongs to the measured population, task and outcome. One speaker describes a controlled developer experiment reporting a 55.8% speedup on a templated JavaScript HTTP-server task with twelve visible tests. That reported result does not establish the same gain across engineering work, labor removal or autonomous production delivery; the underlying study was not independently reviewed in the supplied evidence. Likewise, a percentage of AI-attributed pull requests needs its period, total population and attribution definition; participation is not the same as no-human-intervention delivery. [5] [6]

Check the measuring instrument

A score can change because the grader changes, not because the agent improves. A math answer parser can lose a minus sign or mishandle a fraction. In a security demonstration, generated SQL contained the target flag but the grader checked the wrong representation; a without-skill run also produced the flag. The application still had unresolved vulnerabilities, so neither the grading difference nor the flag establishes a secure application or a reliable comparative advantage. Inspect artifacts and grader inputs before explaining a score as model ability. [7] [8] [9]

Evidence, assurance and maturity are different

A high aggregate score estimates performance under the evaluation conditions; it does not certify each output or prove a rule is never violated. For a financial value that must be correct, an independently implemented check against a trusted canonical source can withhold a value it cannot verify. That assurance remains conditional on the inputs, parser, rules and implementation being correct; deterministic does not mean infallible. A design's brand fit may resist a simple unit test, although explicit design rules can check parts of it and a human can decide whether a failed rule should be changed. [10] [11] [12] [13] [14]

A maturity score answers a different question again. One proposed rubric distinguishes a capability that is absent, measurable, or tracked and analyzed over time; manual measurement can count. Its levels describe management practice, not a calibrated probability that a task succeeds. A polished results interface likewise does not by itself enable controlled comparisons or reveal production failures. [15] [16] [17]

A working prototype is useful for demonstrating a possibility and giving colleagues something concrete to inspect, but a selected demonstration does not establish reliability over a real workload. Conversely, if the sample or measurement cannot support a consequential numerical decision, say the evidence is insufficient and gather more or defer the claim. A speaker's reluctance to report an A/B statistic for a 50-user product is an illustration, not a universal sample-size cutoff. [18] [19]

Design comparisons around a decision

Illustrative example: A team wants to know whether conversation summarization preserves a support agent's quality while reducing context use. Run the same cases against the existing policy and the candidate, holding the model, prompt, tools and starting conditions fixed. Agree in advance on what counts as acceptable resolution and which cost and quality tradeoffs matter. Use a sensible baseline, repeated trials, and cases long enough to trigger summarization; record whether it actually ran. Otherwise a tied score could simply compare two runs in which the intervention never activated. Even with a trigger check, a handful of trials is suggestive, not a precise ranking. [20] [21] [22]

Pre-registering cases, scoring, conditions and directional predictions makes it easier to distinguish a planned test from a result chosen afterward. A proposed historical-persona study fixes its instrument before collection and tests a hypothesis on a different figure from the one that motivated it; its predicted benefit of documentary anchoring remains a prediction, not a completed result. Pre-registration and controls constrain some biases, but neither proves a hypothesis. [23] [24]

Look at changes by case and criterion, not just the average: an agent may improve ordinary resolutions while regressing on sensitive refunds or becoming less clear. Those rows can change a release decision even when a headline score rises. For how to set the decision's case mix, see Design evaluation distributions for decisions; for investigation of individual traces, see Tracing, failure analysis and evaluator methods. [25]

Interpret variation and missing counterfactuals

Zero temperature chooses the highest-scoring token at each step, but underlying scores and system behavior can still vary; a perfectly repeated answer can also repeat an error. Repeated model rollouts expose variation in trajectories, whereas different task environments introduce another source of uncertainty. Running the same easy environment many times cannot substitute for sampling relevant environments. Confidence intervals should respect the task-and-environment hierarchy, and their coverage—the frequency with which the procedure contains the target across repeated samples—needs checking rather than being assumed from the nominal label. One speaker reports poor coverage for base-case-only intervals, but does not supply the simulation setup or estimator implementation. [26] [27] [28]

More repetitions also cannot recover an unobserved counterfactual. A cafe agent inferred that existing opening hours were best because there were no sales outside them; the cafe had never opened then, so those observations say nothing direct about demand under extended hours. This is missing support for an alternative policy. A different problem is confounding between observed groups: an observational human-versus-agent pull-request comparison may reflect easier work assigned to agents. Checking pull-request size is useful, but equal size does not establish equal requirements, risk or review; authorship signals and reverts are proxies too. Neither example supports a causal ranking. [29] [30] [31]

Survey percentages have their own population boundary. One presenter reports 1,048 respondents in a builder-heavy survey, without a sampling frame establishing representation of all organizations. The result describes those respondents; it cannot establish universal adoption or that the most common practice is adequate. Changing survey composition also prevents assuming a year-to-year change describes the same organizations. [32]
