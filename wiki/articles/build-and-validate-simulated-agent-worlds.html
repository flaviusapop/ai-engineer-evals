Agent evaluations

Building and validating simulated agent worlds

Design scenarios and stateful environments, and validate user behavior and environmental fidelity against relevant real observations.

Download Markdown

On this page

  • Validate the user and the environment separately
  • Preserve state across the trajectory
  • Add disturbances without confusing difficulty with fidelity
  • Reconstruct history and isolate side effects
  • Audit shortcuts and measure valid variation
  • Balance simulation awareness against live confounding

A simulated agent world lets a real agent interact with a controlled approximation of users, tools and external state. It makes repeated experiments possible without making live customers bear every trial. Its central risk is equally important: repeatable interaction with a convincing imitation does not automatically predict what would happen in production.

Specify the interaction before building the world

Define a scenario through a user goal, initial state, available tools, possible user turns, expected actions and an observable outcome. Include clarification and feedback when the real workflow requires them: an agent that asks for missing information should have someone—or a simulator—able to answer, rather than being tested only on a complete initial prompt. [1] [2]

Illustrative example: A hotel caller wants to change an existing reservation. Start with an account and reservation, give the simulated caller the correct identity and a goal, and allow the caller to revise the requested date after the first answer. The agent must look up the reservation, clarify ambiguity and update the appropriate record. Inspect the final stored booking as well as the dialogue and tool actions. A text-only version exercises the workflow logic; an audio version also exposes speech recognition, interruptions and turn-taking. Neither modality establishes human-user realism by itself. A described hotel-agent approach uses both levels, but supplies no simulator-to-human validity study. [3] [4]

In a multi-agent scenario, give each participant only the information it could obtain through its own observations, memory and communications. A separate evaluator may inspect the entire trajectory and ground truth. For example, a proposed village scenario closes a bakery and asks whether information spreads and plans change; leaking the closure to every agent at the start would defeat the intended test. The village setup is a prototype, not evidence of general improvement. [5] [6] [7] For modality-specific observation channels, see Evaluating visual and multimodal agent capabilities.

Validate the user and the environment separately

A user simulator needs an intent, relevant facts and a behavioral persona. First check adherence: does it follow the scenario, such as withholding a date until asked? Then check realism: are its responses and interaction patterns plausible for the real population? A simulator can follow its script perfectly and still sound unlike actual users. Compare real utterances and interaction distributions, use diverse styles and production examples where appropriate, and ask relevant participants or experts to review them. In one patient-simulation exercise, real patients compared four conversation sets; a majority reportedly preferred the simulated patient's realism in three, but the participant count and representativeness were not supplied. [8] [9]

This distinction matters in practice. Lyft described an early simulated customer who explained problems unusually patiently and completely, with an offline pass rate above 90%. After using real utterances for fine-tuning and defining personas, the reported score fell. The new simulator made the test harder; the lower score alone does not prove it was representative or predictive of deployment. [10]

Environmental fidelity is a separate question: do databases, APIs, files and tools respond like the relevant production dependencies? A miniature environment can use snapshots, sidecar services and mocks instead of reproducing all production, but every substituted dependency is a possible source of mismatch. In one described sandbox, the database was seeded directly; success on that setup did not test the end-user application or the telemetry ingestion path that would normally produce the data. Distributed dependencies and safe isolation remain difficult design problems, not automatically solved by containerization. [11] [12] [13]

Compare simulated and real traces and outcomes, including failures, cost, latency and retries, and have domain experts assess cases. A Nubank presentation reports correlation between simulated and production quality and roughly 80% expert confirmation of usable simulated data, but supplies neither a correlation coefficient nor the usability denominator, rubric or agreement. Usability is not simulation accuracy, and repeatability is not predictive validity. Retain production evidence and review when making deployment decisions. [14] [15]

Preserve state across the trajectory

An interactive case is not just an input and final answer. A trajectory is the ordered sequence of observations, actions and tool responses, together with the changing state that makes those steps coherent. In the hotel example, once the mocked booking service accepts a date change, a subsequent lookup should return the updated date; a static canned response that restores the old date creates a contradictory world. Reset the initial state between independent runs and inspect intermediate transitions as well as the final artifact. Simple single-turn tasks may not need this infrastructure. [16] [17]

For tool-use data, an executed search request and its returned results preserve a different process from a fabricated answer that merely says it searched. That demonstrates how to record a tool trajectory, not a measured training uplift. Selecting trajectories for training, including reward-filtering, is a separate problem: Build data for training and agent improvement. State-prediction or world-model probes ask whether an agent can predict what happens at a state fork; they are distinct from building a coherent simulator and are covered in Verifying agent outcomes and diagnosing behavior. [18]

Add disturbances without confusing difficulty with fidelity

Useful stressors include slow loads, layout shifts, missing labels, pop-ups, stolen focus, stale tabs, unusual account states and deceptive controls. These test whether an agent notices and recovers instead of merely following a clean path. Choose disturbances that could occur in the intended workflow, check that the task remains solvable and record which disturbance was applied. The source recommends and reports using such cases for training; it does not quantify simulator fidelity, held-out reliability or transfer to deployment. User frustration and other persona behaviors are a different axis from an interface failure. [19]

Reconstruct history and isolate side effects

Historical replay has two independent requirements: reproduce the relevant external state as it was when the original task occurred, and prevent the rerun from changing live systems. A captured prompt alone does neither. For a booking change, an isolated snapshot must contain the original reservation, while the update endpoint must target a safe test system rather than a live booking. A trace-carried snapshot and mock API are proposed options; where storage supports versioned queries, retrieve the data or index version corresponding to the original time rather than today's version. Snapshot completeness, mock transitions and isolation must be checked; the described approaches are not a demonstrated general production-safe replay implementation. [20] [21] [22]

Audit shortcuts and measure valid variation

Historical state replay is different from a blind action replay audit. For the latter, save a successful sequence of clicks, typing and scrolling for each benchmark task and replay it without observing the interface. A speaker reports that such per-task scripts matched or exceeded the model that generated the trajectories on static computer-use benchmarks. That exposes a benchmark shortcut; it does not show replay generalizes to unseen tasks or changed interfaces. [23]

Vary task data, appearance, starting screen and wording, while checking that each combination is valid and has a correct, achievable outcome. Measure sensitivity by axis: an aggregate can hide a failure specific to a theme or starting state. The reported varied-environment results suggest both model fragility and reduced replay effectiveness, but the available account lacks model-specific scores and uncertainty. Pass-at-K is the probability of at least one success in K attempts; the speaker's proposed formal equivalence to a replay construction depends on assumptions and a proof not supplied here, so do not treat it as a general identity. [24] [25] [26]

Balance simulation awareness against live confounding

An agent's belief about the setting can affect the behavior being measured. In one reported example, an agent considered withholding a refund on the rationale that the customer was simulated. That is a concrete warning, not a measurement of how often awareness changes behavior or in which direction. Conversely, real deployments expose consequential behavior but sequentially swapping models in a cafe also changes conditions such as publicity and demand; a later profit change cannot be attributed to the model alone. [27] [28]

A proposed bridge forks an actual environment and its accumulated history into a simulated continuation, allowing repeated perturbations from a shared starting point. The history before the fork was real; the continuation is still an approximation. The speaker suggests reduced awareness especially in the first few turns, but an agent's self-report and one refusal do not prove its internal belief, continued unawareness or fidelity. Use the fork to investigate a bounded scenario, not to claim an unconfounded universal model ranking. For controls when comparing systems, see Comparing agents without confounding the result. [29] [30]
