A useful evaluation system returns decision-relevant feedback soon enough to act on it, while retaining coverage of consequential failures. Faster execution is not itself a better model, and an evaluation that runs successfully is not evidence that its output is factually acceptable. Budget the checking system as a workload in its own right: generation, scoring, human review, infrastructure and the time needed to revise tests.

## Shorten the feedback loop without changing the claim

Parallel rollouts can reduce wall-clock waiting by executing independent cases concurrently, subject to capacity and cost. They do not make the agent more competent. In one demonstration the parallel benchmark did not finish, so it establishes neither a completed score nor a measured speedup. A separate team reports simulation compressing feedback that previously took days or weeks to hours or less than a day; exact timing and simulation-to-production correlation were not established. Use simulation to screen changes, not as automatic proof of live performance. [cite:jRCpXUjz4CI:c09] [cite:KMR_RBoCa4M:c04]

Measure feedback delay, coverage, annotation quality and infrastructure spend separately. A self-service annotation platform may lower handling costs and shorten loops, but its reported savings did not establish label reliability or judge accuracy. Similarly, a monitor capacity ablation can reveal unnecessary expense: one speaker reported a four-times expansion sparse autoencoder matching a 32-times version in one synthetic SQL-injection backdoor setting with a 40% poison fraction, one model and four tested middle layers. No adaptive attacker was tested, so the comparison does not establish general production-monitoring coverage. Recheck detection of the failures that matter before reducing capacity. [cite:bMjlRrWjdT0:c10] [cite:IQkVMvXQKLY:c08]

## Select and stage checks around decisions

Start with the decision: what failures would block a release, require a fix, or change a model choice? Select the smallest set of signals that covers those failure modes, not the smallest count of metrics. A rare severe failure can justify a dedicated check even if most cases pass. [cite:JsCCrBF7F1g:c09] [cite:UGuNt2_Oz8s:c12]

Run cheap, relevant regression subsets while iterating, then the broader suite at integration. Categorizing cases by failure mode helps select a meaningful subset; it does not guarantee early detection of regressions outside it. The suggested schedule is a cost-control proposal, not a measured miss rate or universal cadence. Where a recurring convention error can be expressed in a maintained lint rule, weigh the rule's authoring and maintenance against repeated review and correction; cheap rule generation does not eliminate maintenance. [cite:ObTPqBGsEbA:c13] [cite:E3KbFLAGD6A:c02]

Check artifacts as soon as a defect is observable. In a video workflow, comparing starting frames can catch identity drift before generating clips; a bad clip can be repaired before a long assembly. Still evaluate the assembled sequence for temporal or story-level defects that a frame check cannot see. This is a workflow argument, not a quantified savings result. [cite:b_PmGocP4rc:c03]

For objective structure, use code before a model judge. **Illustrative:** parse a text-to-JSON response, check required fields and reject nulls; only then spend semantic-review capacity on whether the values are supported by the source. Passing the schema is not factual acceptance. Likewise, known-pattern filters may precede a classifier, but regex cannot provide comprehensive prompt-injection protection. [cite:JsCCrBF7F1g:c05] [cite:1tpqdglI3T0:c09]

## Account for the whole evaluation bill

One case can involve an application call and several judge calls; repeated stochastic trials multiply both. Include generation, judges, expert labels, audits, engineering, compute for checkpoints, and renewing stale tests. Do not reduce sample sizes merely to make the bill look smaller: the remaining comparison must still be informative for the decision. The Game Arena speakers report roughly 400,000 poker hands for a stochastic comparison and discuss pairings to reduce games; they do not provide a sample-size derivation or significance threshold. [cite:UGjk3Uk0MxM:c12] [cite:q4Tr-DknG2M:c09] [cite:Ubwb6NzegyA:c10]

Save outputs and their relevant context so a rubric edit can be tested by rescoring fixed material rather than rerunning the application; a generation prompt change generally requires new outputs. Compare judge decisions against domain-expert labels and investigate disagreements as possible evaluator errors, not just application errors. A writing benchmark's speaker reported over $2,000 for a rubric change versus over $4,000 for a generation-input change, specific to its approximately 120 models, ten tasks and five drafts—not a general price schedule. [cite:UGjk3Uk0MxM:c11] [cite:_dTF3NpPzGo:c09]

A cheap initial answer can also have an expensive audit. One reported case put checking—including second opinions, disagreement resolution and audit—at roughly four times first-answer cost; inconsistent currency captions prevent reconstructing its exact bill. Keep this evaluation budget distinct from the deployed cost per accepted task discussed in [[decision-economics]]. [cite:Fa3hQVIKShw:c07]

## Monitor live behavior according to risk

Offline regression fixtures protect against known failures; production sampling discovers cases the fixtures miss. A cheap regex can track aggregate frustration trends, while a specialized classifier or model judge can inspect more nuanced cases. Neither a high pass rate nor a mature system alone proves an evaluator can be retired: consider severity and what a missed case costs. One workshop suggests 5–10% judge sampling after establishing a baseline, but gives no statistical-power or rare-event justification; it is an example, not a safe default. [cite:-aM2EDTiaMs:c04] [cite:UGuNt2_Oz8s:c07] [cite:ZdheJTfLu-s:c11]

A cascade may send obvious cases through cheap checks and escalate uncertain or high-impact cases to deeper analysis. Audit a sample of bypassed cases, including known incidents and adversarial or changed inputs, and measure misses by severity. Similarity and creator-metadata bypasses have been proposed for video analysis, but their false-negative rates were not reported. A low-cost first pass can silently remove the very context an expensive reviewer needs. [cite:jNE8No-wvok:c12] [cite:4UtrRVtUN_g:c04] [cite:4UtrRVtUN_g:c11]

## Decide whether a specialist judge pays for itself

A small distilled judge is a separate investment from training the model being judged. Compare its held-out agreement with expert labels and the existing committee, failure modes, feedback latency, per-item serving cost, and the cost of collecting labels, training, deployment and replicas. At low volume, the existing committee may be cheaper overall; at high volume or when feedback must sit close to generation, a specialist may justify the up-front work. A video team preferred a smaller judge over a reportedly better but slower larger one; it supplied neither a quantitative accuracy comparison nor a universal break-even threshold. [cite:b_PmGocP4rc:c04] [cite:b_PmGocP4rc:c12]

**Illustrative cost calculation:** if a committee costs $0.10 per item, a specialist costs $0.02 per item, and curation, training and deployment cost $800, the simple financial break-even is 10,000 items: $800 divided by $0.08 saved per item. This excludes ongoing maintenance, replication, labeling and the cost of additional errors; a cheaper judge that misses consequential failures may not be acceptable at any volume.
