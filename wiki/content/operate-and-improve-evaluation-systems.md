An evaluation system is not finished when its judge produces a score. The score must help someone decide what to inspect or change, and the judge itself can become inaccurate as tasks, user behavior, and standards change. Operate it as a monitored component, not as a source of unquestionable ground truth.

## Turn verdicts into bounded action

Keep the dimension, verdict, and reason together. An advertisement can be acceptable for brand safety yet factually inaccurate; a single fail would conceal which property needs repair. A rater's explanation can point a developer toward the offending claim or missing context. A model-generated explanation is useful evidence to inspect, not proof that the verdict or the explanation is correct. [cite:xyL2Ltkh-SA:c05] [cite:GgLQ02aO-hs:c09]

Use that feedback to direct a specific revision, then assess the revised artifact again. A refund agent, for example, can be checked against the store's eligibility rubric, rather than a vague helpfulness criterion. Set a maximum number of attempts and an escalation or stop condition; a retry loop consumes resources and can repeatedly optimize against the same faulty grader. The refund example does not establish grader accuracy or convergence. A separate demonstration uses an overall session rubric and bounded retries, but its prior financial scorecard was not independently verified, and its session-level outcome was not an individual grade for each delegated subtask. [cite:PXj0p_mW9nI:c03] [cite:FGftGOzfohc:c05] [cite:FGftGOzfohc:c12]

## Spend human attention on disagreement—and on passes

Use disagreements between the agent, verifiers, or distinct judges to prioritize cases where a domain expert can resolve ambiguity or find a defective criterion. Production signals such as a possible frustrated follow-up can also find candidates, but a flag is not a confirmed failure. One support-workshop example interprets a follow-up using conversation history; it was staged and did not validate the disagreement label. [cite:Ib5t2RLtxvM:c15] [cite:BOCjybvJYM4:c04]

Do not spend the whole review budget on flagged cases. In one vendor case study, all reported errors among 200 held-out families fell within six disagreements, but a separate example had four model families share the same wrong sign convention. In a generated clinical-note exercise, the presenter reported serious errors in one in five apparent clean passes, often omissions; its denominator, labeling method, configuration and uncertainty were not supplied. Neither result is a general error rate. Together they explain why agreement and passing verdicts also deserve sampled review, including targeted checks for known shared blind spots. A selectively reviewed queue cannot by itself estimate overall prevalence. [cite:Fa3hQVIKShw:c09] [cite:Fa3hQVIKShw:c10] [cite:yqF6XhzbWBk:c08]

Human reviewers should also use the product and inspect complete traces, not just the judge's final label. An apparently poor answer may arise from failed retrieval, a bad tool call, or reasoning after a correct tool result; the repair differs. For the distinction between agreement and truth, see [[agreement-truth-and-the-limits-of-evaluator-proxies]]. [cite:hd7TOvmyAxU:c08] [cite:jNE8No-wvok:c11]

## Maintain a changing judge without losing comparability

Regularly compare sampled human and judge decisions and inspect reasons, including examples from new user populations and failure types. Record the task, input distribution, judge/model and rubric versions with results. When experts reveal a missing criterion, update the rubric and relevant examples, then revalidate against independently held-out judgments reflecting the new standard. These are practical safeguards, not a prescribed numerical threshold: the cited teams did not disclose a universal sampling rate, agreement target, or complete calibration protocol. [cite:xyL2Ltkh-SA:c06] [cite:3z2uT5aDx_Y:c07]

A stable rubric is useful for comparing implementations; an evolving rubric is necessary when the old one misses what matters. Preserve the old version or run both versions on a common set when feasible, and label the break: a score increase after changing the definition does not on its own show the agent improved. A clinical-note presenter reported qualitatively better performance from a judge updated with expert feedback on the same generated-note dataset, but did not provide split hygiene, exact scores or prospective outcomes. Treat the loop as a hypothesis to validate, not proof that iterative feedback generalizes. [cite:yqF6XhzbWBk:c13] [cite:BOCjybvJYM4:c12]

## Select and improve the judge on its actual task

Start with a clear property and a simple baseline judge on relevant traces. Inspect its errors with domain reviewers; test whether a capable model can do the task before trying cheaper models and adapting their instructions. Compare candidates on independent human-labeled cases, including important error types and cost, not on a generic leaderboard alone. A reported legal trace-judging collaboration used this capability-first approach, but disclosed neither detailed cost accounting nor judge-agreement data. Prompt inspection makes a proposed change reviewable; it does not validate it. [cite:bMjlRrWjdT0:c06] [cite:bMjlRrWjdT0:c08] [cite:CvRngaQZQ3Y:c04]

Feedback volume depends on the task and what is being trained. One vendor describes small batches of roughly 10–20 human examples to improve a judge and larger production feedback sets for reward models; that is not a universal sample-size rule. Keep examples used to optimize the judge or agent separate from the independent evaluation used to decide promotion. Otherwise a system may learn to satisfy its own reviewer while both repeat the same mistake. See [[choose-and-combine-judge-methods]] for method selection and [[protect-holdouts-and-historical-evidence]] for holdout protection. [cite:X6NShR2ccOg:c10] [cite:OA-Mc60Rboo:c11]
