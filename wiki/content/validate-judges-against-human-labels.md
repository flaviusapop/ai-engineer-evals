A judge can score thousands of answers consistently and still apply the wrong standard. Validate it as a classifier against human judgments for the intended task, then inspect the kinds of errors it makes. Agreement with those labels is useful evidence of alignment—not proof of truth, task success or reliable predicted probabilities.

## Error costs, precision and recall for the stated positive class

First define what counts as positive. Suppose positive means a **failure that should be flagged**. A true positive is a real failure flagged by the judge; a false positive is a flag on an acceptable answer; a false negative is a failure the judge misses. Then:

- **Precision** = true positives / (true positives + false positives): of the flags, how many are real failures?
- **Recall** = true positives / (true positives + false negatives): of the real failures, how many did the judge flag?

**Illustrative example:** On a labeled set, a judge flags 12 answers, 9 of which are actual failures. Three more failures go unflagged. Precision is 9/12 = 75%; recall is 9/12 = 75%. The denominators happen to match here but measure different populations. If a denominator is zero, report the metric as undefined rather than inventing a percentage. Show counts, prevalence and uncertainty alongside rates: an aggregate agreement score can conceal a failure class that the judge rarely catches. [cite:qZj7sqmidqA:c14] [cite:NKwIX3CiRgU:c05]

Choose an operating point according to the downstream action. When a flag sends a case for review and a miss reaches users, greater recall may be worth extra false alarms. When a flag itself triggers a costly accusation, precision may matter more. These are conditional choices, not rules for an entire field. One workshop first uses cancer screening to motivate recall and later describes medical use as favoring the opposite; its captions do not resolve that tension. Specify the positive label, the consequence of each error and who bears it instead of silently choosing one remark. [cite:Xfl50508LZM:c11]

## Establish the human reference

Select raters who represent the relevant users or have the required domain expertise. Give them a common rubric and concrete borderline cases, and examine where people disagree before treating a single adjudicated label as a reference. Ask whether the disagreement is due to missing context, ambiguous instructions or a genuine difference in values. Revise the criteria or record the disagreement and its resolution; do not assume that additional labels alone settle an incoherent standard. Human–human agreement and judge–human agreement are different measurements, and neither makes a subjective judgment automatically true. There is no universal human-agreement threshold established by these sources. [cite:xyL2Ltkh-SA:c04] [cite:hqHC6Z_lXyo:c04]

Have humans label cases independently of the judge's verdict before comparing them, so the model's answer does not become the reference by default. Use multiple raters where practical; a single knowledgeable rater can be a starting point but leaves variation less visible. For collection and adjudication details, see [[collect-and-interpret-human-judgments]]. [cite:obfPzbapMIM:c13]

## Validate against held-out labels

Collect representative positive and negative cases, including relevant difficult slices. Retain the application outputs and context so judge revisions can be tested on the same material without rerunning the application. Separate examples used for prompt development and iteration from independently labeled held-out cases used for the final estimate. Run the frozen judge on each held-out case with the permissible task context and intended reference facts, but withhold the held-out human verdict and adjudication. Have the judge generate its verdict independently before comparing it with the human label; otherwise it could copy the answer instead of judging the case. [cite:NdrSPm6NCdk:c09] Report the confusion counts, precision, recall and overall agreement, the sample and label process, and uncertainty or limitations. Revisit representativeness as the application and traffic change. [cite:UGjk3Uk0MxM:c11] [cite:obfPzbapMIM:c13]

Inspect disagreements in both directions. Read the case and judge rationale: a false alarm can expose an overstrict judge, while a missed failure may reveal a rubric gap, missing context or insufficient model capability. The human label can also be wrong. Resolve the actual issue, update the development set or instructions as appropriate, and evaluate the revised judge on fresh held-out data rather than repeatedly tuning to the reported test. A team whose critic excused real bugs found it useful to read full traces and adjust the critic where its judgments diverged from humans; that account is qualitative rather than a quantified reliability study. [cite:qZj7sqmidqA:c12] [cite:mR-WAvEPRwE:c10]

For contrast, a browser-verifier experiment described roughly 50 trajectories for development and about 100 held out, with two paid annotations per held-out trajectory. The speakers reported false positives falling from almost half to zero on their gold evaluation and a kappa of 0.58. Confusion-matrix counts, uncertainty and full adjudication details were not supplied, so this is not evidence of zero false positives in general. [cite:xLxhT2ZI7UM:c07] [cite:xLxhT2ZI7UM:c08]

A different actionability workshop used approximately random purported human labels. Its displayed disagreement and precision/recall numbers illustrate mechanics only; they do not validate the judge. Another workshop's 46% agreement likewise used deliberately random labels. Do not promote either demonstration result into a quality estimate. [cite:Xfl50508LZM:c10] [cite:qZj7sqmidqA:c12]

## Probe bias, not just the average

To test presentation sensitivity, swap the order of paired answers while keeping their content fixed and compare verdicts; blind model identity where possible; and inspect slices for preference for length, confidence, style or model family. A reversal under an order swap is a diagnostic, not a complete bias benchmark. Blinding names cannot remove all stylistic cues, and using a different judge family or provider does not guarantee independent errors. A small same-family demonstration also confounded origin with wording, so it does not establish the magnitude of a family effect. [cite:NdrSPm6NCdk:c06] [cite:NdrSPm6NCdk:c08] [cite:qZj7sqmidqA:c14]

Finally, separate three questions: **human–judge agreement** asks whether verdicts match a chosen reference; **precision and recall** characterize classifier errors for a specified positive class; **probability calibration** asks whether stated probabilities correspond to observed frequencies. A high value on one is not proof of the others. For what agreement can and cannot establish, see [[agreement-truth-and-the-limits-of-evaluator-proxies]]; for confidence calibration, see [[compare-systems-confidence-and-behavior-without-mixing-estimands]].
