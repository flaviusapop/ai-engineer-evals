A team can generate a change quickly and still be unable to ship it responsibly. The limiting resource may be a reviewer who understands the affected system, a slow build, or evidence that the change is safe enough for its consequences. Decide review effort per change, not per author: automation can prepare and sometimes route routine work, while people retain responsibility for consequential decisions and for the organization’s understanding of the system.

## Route scrutiny by consequence

Start with what the change can affect, how reversible a failure would be, and the evidence available. A disposable side project and a long-lived system with coupled components need not have the same review policy. A reasonable proposed default is close human inspection of authentication, permissions, money movement and irreversible data; split a large critical change into reviewable units where possible. This is guidance, not an exhaustive or validated risk classification. [cite:Ib5GBkD555M:c02] [cite:ZpK5PWX2YRM:c01] [cite:ZpK5PWX2YRM:c02]

For a bounded mechanical change, automated checks and review can run first, with the author testing and reading the result before requesting another person’s attention. One team describes requiring automated review comments to be addressed before human review; resolving those comments does not prove correctness. A different workflow prescreened stale-feature-flag cleanups using affected modules, variants and experiment state, backtested the scoring against historical work, and reported seven live PRs with green CI. The scoring thresholds and error rates were not supplied; this small result supports a bounded experiment, not unrestricted autonomous merging. [cite:I2LL_wd89-A:c07] [cite:bdHaOXZOhcM:c08] [cite:6MudaeKdBSk:c07] [cite:6MudaeKdBSk:c08]

Make the organization’s tolerance explicit: some teams demand stronger pre-merge assurance; others accept certain recoverable defects in exchange for faster releases and repair. Add automated approval or blocking incrementally, with named escalation for unclassified or exceptional changes. Fewer human comments may indicate a mature reviewer, but could also reflect disengagement or missed defects; silence is not a safety measurement. The suggested shift of human attention toward architecture and product intent as automation improves is a direction to evaluate, not evidence that human approval can already be removed. [cite:s-aixZYJG4c:c02] [cite:s-aixZYJG4c:c10] [cite:7b9tov5Uc5s:c05] [cite:EL123UNokkI:c13]

## Preserve learning, not just defect detection

Review also distributes knowledge: people learn why a design was chosen, mentor one another, discover conflicting assumptions and build a shared architectural vocabulary. An automated correctness check does not itself transfer that understanding. If fewer people inspect routine diffs, deliberately replace the lost conversation with design reviews, walkthroughs or shared ownership of decisions—not merely a faster approval button. [cite:s-aixZYJG4c:c01] [cite:YgEv7IQzGdM:c01]

One team’s experience illustrates the tradeoff: after tooling and notifications made design documents more visible, generated documents flooded reviewers, so it required people to read documents before shipping them. That redirects attention to durable intent, but it creates review work of its own; it is a reported team practice, not proof that design review universally replaces code review. [cite:AMiyLItEtLA:c02]

## Make the reviewer useful and independently accountable

A proposed minimal pull-request reviewer could run in a sandbox, receive a repository webhook with the change, and post feedback through the review API. This is a starting architecture, not a demonstrated working loop or a competent or secure reviewer by itself: the harness still needs appropriate context, affordable execution, useful comments and evaluation of missed findings. [cite:4UtrRVtUN_g:c01]

Give the reviewer the diff, relevant surrounding code and explicit team standards. Current code shows what exists; an approved architecture or dependency policy states what *should* exist and may intentionally differ. Supply such knowledge before generation as well, then check direction during implementation rather than waiting until the finished PR. One speaker suggests checkpoints around quarter, half and three-quarter completion; these are illustrative, not an established optimal cadence. Current documentation and compact reference applications can also help when model knowledge is stale, but examples do not guarantee a correct integration. [cite:VrpEyglYgeU:c05] [cite:YqxpPI7YUjE:c04] [cite:YqxpPI7YUjE:c08] [cite:juoNbJiZUi0:c01] [cite:juoNbJiZUi0:c02]

Separate implementation from assessment. A fresh reviewer context can focus on the change and standards rather than inherit the builder’s exploration and confidence. A different model family, harness or checking method may expose different blind spots; agreement is still not proof, and fresh contexts are not statistically independent by definition. A builder declaring its own tests and work complete hides rather than eliminates the verification decision. [cite:LlgiOCmFG_w:c06] [cite:YqxpPI7YUjE:c05] [cite:4UtrRVtUN_g:c06] [cite:ZpK5PWX2YRM:c07]

Neither a human signature nor a reviewer bot is automatically independent judgment. An overloaded person can defer to a confident AI suggestion, including an incorrect one. A cited study about agreement with AI is not itself a code-review-specific validation, but it motivates a practical safeguard: give reviewers time and access to the underlying change and evidence, and ask them to state why a consequential decision is acceptable rather than merely acknowledge the bot’s conclusion. [cite:03l29gJXpCE:c04] [cite:LQW_Wvqhp04:c03]

## Govern authority and deliver actionable findings

Specify which findings block, which require a named human, who can approve, and what happens on uncertainty. Policies must consider interactions as well as isolated diffs: two individually plausible concurrent changes can jointly break a shared contract. The proposed graph-level approach does not supply a tested conflict-resolution procedure. Also protect the reviewer’s mandate from conversational pressure: a hypothetical author repeatedly asking an approval-capable reviewer to examine only cleanup could divert it from a security checklist. This is an illustrative threat, not a measured bypass rate. [cite:s-aixZYJG4c:c07] [cite:BgLdvF7Qkh8:c05]

Treat generated findings as candidates. Check validity and relevance, categorize and deduplicate overlapping reports, then send actionable issues to the right audience. Uber describes such post-processing for multiple reviewer outputs, without publishing a calibrated precision or recall effect for each stage. A security vendor similarly describes adversarial validation and noise gates, but does not provide the ground truth needed to establish their effectiveness. [cite:EL123UNokkI:c03] [cite:UW8Qkk7MG8A:c03]

Filtering is a tradeoff, not a contest to minimize comments: suppressing noise can also suppress important findings. A technically defensible nit may waste a human’s attention or cause a coding agent to add unnecessary defensive code. An agent may tolerate more minor comments than a person, yet inaccurate feedback in its fix-review loop can reverse prior work or make it oscillate. Match the presentation to the audience while checking the quality and stability of the resulting changes. Build review targets from recurring local incident classes, and explicitly exclude low-value suggestions where appropriate. [cite:4UtrRVtUN_g:c02] [cite:4UtrRVtUN_g:c03] [cite:EL123UNokkI:c11] [cite:EL123UNokkI:c12]

Some standards demand judgment even with good context. For example, a small feature that expands a state machine from three states to ten might deserve scrutiny; a universal four-state limit would be arbitrary. [cite:E3KbFLAGD6A:c08] Policy-specific reviewers can raise concerns about complexity or weak tests before a human examines them, but neither a numeric target nor an agent’s approval proves the design maintainable. [cite:E3KbFLAGD6A:c09]

## Interpret review and quality signals without confusing them

Operational feedback measures adoption and behavior, not all defects found. Uber reports roughly 25,000 review comments per week, feedback on 10% of comments, negative feedback on 4% of PRs, and an overall comment address rate of about 67%; nearly three quarters of high-severity comments were addressed. These numbers use different denominators. Selective feedback and addressed comments cannot establish precision, recall, or the defects that escaped review. [cite:EL123UNokkI:c09]

Triangulate actual outcomes with different limitations. In one observational account, human and agent PR revert rates were low and broadly similar, and reviewer-labeled severity counts also looked broadly similar. Non-reverted defects, task differences and detector errors remain unknown. A separate keyword analysis of reviewer comments reported a roughly 1.5-times human baseline for one agent’s SQL-injection category; that is a comment-derived signal, not an independently audited vulnerability rate. Similar aggregates can coexist with category-specific risks worth investigating. [cite:474j-n1Ltxc:c03] [cite:474j-n1Ltxc:c05] [cite:474j-n1Ltxc:c07]

Static analysis offers another lens, but keep its quantities distinct. Issue density per unit of code is not the same as total findings or total code burden. Cyclomatic complexity reflects branching structure; cognitive complexity is intended to approximate human comprehension difficulty, not functional correctness or a proven maintenance outcome. In a vendor comparison, quality analysis of generated solutions to more than 4,444 Java assignments was juxtaposed with public SWE-bench pass rates from a different benchmark. Those pass rates were not results on the Java corpus, and the presentation did not provide a complete controlled protocol. [cite:NuePCNMpWGc:c02] [cite:NuePCNMpWGc:c03] [cite:NuePCNMpWGc:c04]

## Track review burden and delivery, not generated volume

Review debt is the proposed gap between code produced and code people have reviewed, understood and trust. It can accumulate when output grows faster than the organization can absorb it. One proposed burden score combines deterministic PR signals: diff size and coupling, test-evidence gaps, ownership spread, AI-authorship indicators, and missing rationale or evidence. Reproducible calculation is useful for explaining *why* a change deserves attention, but reproducibility does not establish that the score predicts risk or actual review time. [cite:TJPInBjhE4Q:c01] [cite:TJPInBjhE4Q:c02]

Use the score as a prompt for action: identify the checks that fired, what the reviewer should focus on and what the author can improve. A change touching multiple owner teams may require multiple mental models and coordination; split it where that preserves a coherent change. Authorship markers such as branch names or co-author footers are incomplete signals, not proof of who wrote a change. The proposal inconsistently calls an authorship indicator information-only while assigning it points, so teams should make any weighting explicit rather than silently treating AI provenance as a defect. Backtest rankings against local reviewer experience and begin with nonblocking feedback before setting thresholds; even a good retrospective fit is not proof of future predictive validity. [cite:TJPInBjhE4Q:c04] [cite:TJPInBjhE4Q:c05] [cite:TJPInBjhE4Q:c09] [cite:TJPInBjhE4Q:c07]

Measure the flow from generation through review, CI and release, alongside outcomes after release. Slow builds can encourage developers to bundle otherwise separate changes; this is a proposed mechanism rather than an isolated causal result. One speaker describes reviewers unable to process a large agent-generated submission asking another agent to reimplement it, discarding the original work. That anecdote shows how apparent output can become duplicated effort, though it does not quantify frequency. Smaller, coherent changes and better pipeline capacity can address the bottleneck without pretending that all large changes are wrong. [cite:Se8jHLliLXE:c04] [cite:Ti-BmGU1_mQ:c13]

## Judge mergeability and the investment in future change

Passing tests or looking convincing in a demo answers only part of the question. A patch can satisfy the exercised behavior yet introduce security issues, excessive complexity, weak tests, unrelated changes or coupling that makes later work difficult. One speaker reports maintainers judged only about half of sampled test-passing benchmark PRs mergeable; the agents had no opportunity to iterate on their feedback, and the account does not establish a general mergeability rate. A clean review or test run is evidence about what was checked, not proof that defects are absent. [cite:FvxY8oPoI8o:c10] [cite:_mi3alkqy4s:c03] [cite:Ib5GBkD555M:c03] [cite:4UtrRVtUN_g:c12]

This gap also matters for incentives: if the immediate reward is passing tests, a patch can be rewarded without its longer-term design cost appearing in the signal. The cost of poor architecture may emerge months later and be hard to attribute to one change. Review the design and expected future change paths separately from the immediate task result; do not assume more test-passing attempts automatically solve maintainability. [cite:Ib5GBkD555M:c06] [cite:Ib5GBkD555M:c07]

Technical debt is not always a mistake: a shortcut may deliver value now, while complexity imposes ongoing costs later. Decide whether to refactor by comparing the present business value and maintenance constraints against the cost and risk of change. Waiting for better tools could lower future implementation cost, but also postpones benefits available now. In one medical-document pipeline rebuild, the speaker reports lower processing time and cost, support for larger files and faster feature work; the retrospective does not isolate the architecture change from other factors. Neither immediate rewrite nor indefinite waiting follows automatically from that case. [cite:7vn4WpqNpck:c01] [cite:7vn4WpqNpck:c09]
