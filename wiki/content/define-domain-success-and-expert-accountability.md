A domain evaluation begins with a decision about usable work: what must a person be able to do with the output, what happens if it is wrong, and who is responsible for acting on it? A benchmark score can inform that decision, but neither a high score nor a cited answer establishes that the workflow is safe, effective or ready to deploy.

## Define acceptance around the actual work

Specify the completed artifact or decision, not merely whether an answer contains some correct facts. A financial statement with an incorrect cost or income line may have substantial partial credit yet still require the analyst to redo the work. For that use, count a fully usable statement separately from partial accuracy. For an index whose source documents can be revisited, an imperfect extraction may be tolerable; for sensitive insurance or financial extraction, the consequences may justify a more costly, accurate and verified path. These are different operating points, not a universal demand for perfection. [cite:PXj0p_mW9nI:c06] [cite:RQi7x-navxU:c06]

Set acceptance with the customer and responsible expert using the task's consequences, expected exposure and available verification. Even a small error rate can mean many affected people at high volume, but demonstrating rare-error reliability also requires sufficiently representative evidence. A threshold should state the use and population to which it applies, the evidence required, and what happens when evidence or performance is insufficient—not turn one attractive average into a blanket approval. [cite:AN65uc645mE:c10] [cite:3ZlQk4-knWM:c12]

## Measure recovery, not just error counts

Ask what users must do after each failure. A voice assistant playing the wrong song can require a listener to notice, interrupt playback and repeat the request; saying it did not understand imposes a different burden. A confirmation step may prevent a wrong action but also delay correct requests. A visual list selectable by remote may make confirmation easier, while opening the wrong TV channel may disrupt the current state. Estimate the consequences for the actual interface and compare policies on the combined burden, including delay and wrong actions. The source's recovery times are illustrative, not measurements of satisfaction. [cite:Zd5b40Jbp_k:c03] [cite:Zd5b40Jbp_k:c08]

For a writing assistant, revision effort may be more relevant than an abstract style score, but it must be measured with the intended writers and tasks; the cited recommendation did not time human editing. Recovery cost is a useful proxy, not the same thing as user satisfaction or the domain outcome itself. [cite:_dTF3NpPzGo:c10]

Report results by meaningful user group, task difficulty and operating condition as well as overall. For example, separate a straightforward single-table analysis from a multi-table one, and show where the sample is too small to support a decision. A weak or under-evidenced slice may justify a narrower deployment or more evidence even when the aggregate looks good. Difficulty comparisons require appropriate splits and contamination controls; neither a universal minimum sample size nor a guaranteed threshold is supplied here. [cite:3ZlQk4-knWM:c12] [cite:TNwJ1LMiENk:c06]

## Build the workflow with practitioners

Begin with recurrent, tractable work whose quality practitioners can judge. Have them break it into the order of operations, identify exceptions, explain why similar cases deserve different treatment, and review traces as well as final outputs. Convert that knowledge into task instructions, examples and evaluation criteria, then maintain them as the workflow changes. Pair practitioners with engineers and measurement specialists: expert participation supplies domain knowledge, not automatic proof of safety. One reported financial-planning practice lets nonengineers author and maintain workflow skills, but does not provide a controlled effectiveness comparison. [cite:gpmze-6ueGU:c03] [cite:gpmze-6ueGU:c08] [cite:Yphdry8ttAQ:c03] [cite:eAXxdtNlK04:c07]

Organize the work according to what can be measured and how quickly it must iterate. When meaningful objective measures are weak and taste dominates, an expert oracle can directly judge outputs and make changes. When useful measures exist and manual engineering iteration is fast enough, an evaluator can define failure modes and collect quality measures to guide engineers. If manual iteration becomes too slow and suitable automation exists, consider an architect who designs an improvement system that learns or adapts; the role is more than designing the application around domain constraints. These are role choices, not necessarily separate hires. [cite:kfSDc2eVLo4:c01] [cite:kfSDc2eVLo4:c02]

In a reported prior-authorization case, an expert moved from direct review and product changes to defining failure modes and metrics, building a review dashboard and hiring clinicians; their feedback supported engineering changes. Later policy variation made manual fixes too slow and motivated an architect approach. This account does not establish the effectiveness or safety of automated adaptation. One concentrated product may work with a single expert oracle; a clinical scribe spanning specialties and geographies may need distributed reviewers and local ownership. Match expertise to the particular task and add engineering and evaluation skills; the reported staffing cases are practitioner accounts, not controlled evidence for one universal structure. [cite:kfSDc2eVLo4:c05] [cite:kfSDc2eVLo4:c03] [cite:kfSDc2eVLo4:c04] [cite:kfSDc2eVLo4:c08]

## Keep decision authority and evidence together

Separate preparation from authority. In a reported planning workflow, an agent drafts a client's cash-flow or insurance analysis while the planner works elsewhere; the planner reviews and adjusts it and controls sending the recommendation. Evaluate the draft, the handoff and the expert's final decision separately. Specify what can be prepared automatically, what evidence the reviewer sees, when the system must stop or escalate, and who can approve or deliver the result. Human review is a control to test, not an assumption that every error will be caught. This account reports a process, not measured advice safety or time savings. [cite:gpmze-6ueGU:c01] [cite:Tt2kX2sgQio:c10]

Give the responsible expert an inspectable trail from material facts and actions to their source and a domain-native view of what changed. An evaluation evidence pack can record intended use, source lineage, detected failures and fallback, segmented results, operational controls and named owners. It supports a reviewable decision; a provenance link is not proof that the source or inference is correct, and a proposed pack is not a regulatory approval or a mandated file count. [cite:aVbRSIQO2dE:c10] [cite:CLttOU7n6sI:c09] [cite:3ZlQk4-knWM:c10]

## Test domain outcomes, then bound the inference

Build benchmarks around real units of work and the tools and constraints they involve. Use bounded tasks and a consistent scaffold so candidates face comparable conditions; hold out relevant examples and compare against a sensible simple baseline. A speaker reports that linear methods matched or exceeded some costly single-cell models in particular benchmarks; incomplete tables, splits and uncertainty prevent generalizing that result, and the suggested information-loss mechanism was not established by a causal ablation. [cite:3ZMUiFaQ3qg:c02] [cite:-561cZmir5Q:c06] [cite:-561cZmir5Q:c07]

A simulated environment can establish that a system handles the cases modeled there, not that it produces the same benefit in the real workflow. For example, a synthetic remediation exercise used a modeled manual baseline and reported times for resolved cases; its caption interpretation is provisional, and neither the baseline nor those selected cases establish production recovery performance. Likewise, a research competition result is not trading profit. Record the simulated environment, comparator and limits, then seek evidence from representative real use before claiming efficacy. [cite:LrGCT7G_rU8:c07] [cite:kiqubc5b5Yo:c07]
