What the videos collectively teach

1. Start with observed failures, then build the dataset. The recurring practical sequence is to inspect real traces, distinguish symptoms from causes, group failures, and write explicit acceptance criteria. A small, representative golden set is more useful than a large collection of generic questions. Include successful cases, negative tests, and consequential edge cases; otherwise a fix can improve the target failure while breaking existing behavior. Domain experts should contribute explanations and corrections, not just labels. Representative videos: Ship Real Agents, How Evals and Prompts Shape Agent Behavior, and How to Leverage Domain Expertise.

2. Evaluate the evaluator before optimizing against it. Prefer deterministic checks for objectively testable constraints and use semantic judges for questions requiring interpretation. Give judges the evidence their question requires: source-grounded faithfulness is not independent factual correctness, and an agent claiming completion is not proof. Calibrate against expert-labeled, held-out examples, investigate disagreements, and separate outcome completion from partial progress. A polished rubric can still miss important domain errors. Representative videos: Your LLM Judge Is a Confident Liar, Inside 847 Production Clinical AI Notes, and Ship Real Agents.

3. Benchmark the complete agent system, not a model name. Results depend on tools, harness, environment, state, budgets, and verifier quality. Hold these conditions explicit, inspect trajectories, and distinguish infrastructure failures from capability failures. Fresh tasks and protected grading reduce solution leakage; environment variation tests whether behavior generalizes beyond replayable setups. Repeated trials should measure reliability and uncertainty, not merely whether one attempt eventually succeeds. Representative videos: The Art & Science of Benchmarking Agents, SWE-rebench, and Computer Use at the Edge of the Statistical Precipice.

4. Connect offline tests to a production improvement loop. Instrument execution, discover recurring failures, turn them into regression cases, compare candidate changes against a baseline, and validate the result after release. Version datasets and judging criteria alongside prompts, skills, and harness changes. Agents can investigate traces and propose repairs, but humans still own what counts as improvement. Optimizing a proxy such as fewer turns can remove useful documentation lookup or approval steps. Representative videos: Continuously Improving Agents with Langfuse, The Loop Is the Product, and Skill issue: Lessons from skilling up coding agents.

5. Measure useful outcomes under domain-specific risk and cost. Token prices, throughput, and aggregate accuracy are inadequate decision rules. Count acceptable completed work, retries, failed attempts, latency, and review burden. In finance, numerical provenance and replayable calculations matter; in healthcare, missing a dangerous condition may outweigh many ordinary correct responses. Evaluation should therefore reflect the actual workflow and consequences, rather than importing a universal quality score. Representative videos: The Cheaper AI Model That Costs You More, How Kepler Built Verifiable AI for Financial Services, and Shipping AI to a Million Patients Without an A/B Test.

Important disagreements and caveats

- Automation has contested limits. Several workshops operationalize human-calibrated LLM judges; Nick Heiner instead favors professional-human comparisons for nuanced writing. Thais Castello Branco argues that legitimate taste differences should retain their context, not be averaged away. These positions do not establish one universally best grader.
- Production experimentation is not equally acceptable everywhere. The Loop Is the Product emphasizes validating value with users; Ufonia emphasizes simulation and staged clinical evidence before exposure. Simulation can screen changes, but requires validation and does not itself prove real-world safety.
- Demonstrations and proposals are weaker than validated results. Malleable Evals presents an unfinished conceptual implementation. The Ship Real Agents workshop uses randomized demonstration annotations, not genuine calibration evidence. Likewise, task-specific vendor results and leaderboard gains should not become general reliability claims.

Start with these five videos

1. Ship Real Agents: Hands-On Evals for Agentic Applications — Laurie Voss. Foundation: follow traces through error analysis, evaluator selection, experiments, and regression testing. It supplies the overall workflow; treat its randomized annotation exercise as illustration only.
2. How Evals and Prompts Shape Agent Behavior — YouTube Ads. Judge calibration: learn human agreement, explanatory labels, expert audits, and held-out tests. It strengthens the judgment layer before you automate scoring or optimize prompts.
3. SWE-rebench: Lessons from Evaluating Coding Agents — Ibragim Badertdinov. Agent benchmarking: make environments executable, protect against leakage, define retries, and distinguish occasional success from consistent reliability. The concrete coding examples expose general measurement traps.
4. Continuously Improving Agents with Langfuse — Annabell Schäfer & Lotte Verheyden. Production flywheel: connect instrumentation, application-specific signals, human review, and maintained datasets. It shows how the earlier offline methods become ongoing operational work.
5. Shipping AI to a Million Patients Without an A/B Test — Jared Joselowitz. Advanced domain evaluation: study hazard-linked scenarios, simulator and judge validation, harm-sensitive objectives, and staged evidence. It tests whether your evaluation approach remains defensible when mistakes have clinical consequences.
